## Problem Overview

Defected Nuts is an industrial computer vision challenge wrapped in a playful
Willy Wonka storyline. The task places you in the role of a Quality Control
Engineer. Your objective is to process high-resolution (1024×1024) images of
hazelnuts and detect subtle, pixel-level defects such as hairline cracks, tiny
cuts, small holes, and machine print contamination.

The catch? You are only provided with 431 images of perfect, defect-free
hazelnuts for training. The test set contains 70 images, all of which contain
some form of defect, and you must output a precise pixel-level anomaly mask
(heatmap) localizing the damage. Because you have no training examples of what a
defect looks like, nor any ground-truth segmentation masks to train on, this is
a classic Unsupervised Anomaly Detection (UAD) and segmentation problem.

## Clarifying Constraints & The AUPRO Metric

The prompt strictly limits participants to using only a standard ResNet18
pretrained on ImageNet. No other models or external datasets are allowed.

The evaluation metric is AUPRO (Area Under the Per-Region Overlap curve), capped
at a False Positive Rate (FPR) of 30%. Understanding this metric is crucial:
Traditional pixel-wise metrics (like standard AUC) heavily bias towards large
defects. If an anomaly detection system perfectly segments a massive dark stain
but entirely misses a tiny, one-pixel-wide hairline crack, pixel-wise metrics
will still report a high score because the vast majority of defective pixels
were found.

AUPRO fixes this. It calculates the overlap for each independent defect region
separately, normalizing them so that a tiny crack contributes just as much to
your final score as a massive hole. If your heatmap smears over the image or
loses high-resolution spatial details, your AUPRO score will tank. Furthermore,
the 30% FPR cap simulates real-world factory economics: you cannot simply
flag 50% of the normal nut surface as "defective" just to ensure you catch the
cracks, because throwing away good product is too expensive.

## Defining "Normal"

Since we cannot train a model to recognize defects (we don't have any), we must
take the opposite approach: learn exactly what a "normal" hazelnut looks like,
and flag anything that deviates.

Historically, researchers used Autoencoders for this—compressing the image into
a bottleneck and reconstructing it, hoping the model would fail to reconstruct
anomalies. However, Autoencoders often produce blurry outputs and sometimes
generalize too well, accidentally reconstructing the defects. The reference
solution bypasses this entirely using an elegant feature-matching approach
inspired by PatchCore, a state-of-the-art framework for industrial visual
inspection.

## Feature Extraction: Why ResNet18 Mid-Layers?

The solution uses the allowed ResNet18 as a fixed feature extractor (.eval()
mode, no gradients). For every image, we pass it through the network, but we
don't care about the final classification output. Instead, we extract the
intermediate activation maps from the first three residual blocks (layer1,
layer2, and layer3).

Why not layer4? In convolutional networks, early layers capture low-level
textures (edges, color gradients). Mid-layers capture patterns (the rough
texture of a nut shell). Late layers capture high-level semantic meaning (the
concept of a "nut"). For defect detection, semantic meaning is useless—we
already know it's a nut. We need the local, textural descriptors from the
mid-layers to spot abnormal cuts or cracks.

The feature maps from these three layers are all bilinearly resized to a common
spatial grid of 128×128. By concatenating them along the channel dimension (64
+ 128 + 256), we obtain a 448-dimensional vector for every single patch in that
128×128 grid.

for l in [l1, l2, l3]:
    l = F.interpolate(l, size=(128,128), mode="bilinear")

## The Memory Bank & Subsampling

If we extract 128 \times 128 = 16,384 patch descriptors for all 431 training
images, we end up with over 7 million 448-dimensional vectors. Storing all of
these in a "memory bank" of normal features would consume massive amounts of RAM
and make inference impossibly slow.

To solve this, the baseline uses random subsampling. We define a memory budget
(n_keep = 100,000) and randomly sample a fraction (~1.4%) of the normal patches
across the entire training set.

idx = torch.randperm(len(f), device=device)[:round(len(f) * frac)]
bank.append(f[idx])

Note for competitors: While random sampling works well enough to hit the 0.7492
baseline, the original PatchCore paper uses a "k-Center Greedy" algorithm
(coreset subsampling). This algorithm mathematically ensures that the subsampled
bank maintains the maximum possible coverage of the feature space without
redundant overlapping points, which would significantly boost performance over
purely random sampling.

## Anomaly Heatmaps by Distance

At test time, the logic is brilliantly simple. We take a defective image,
extract its 16,384 patches using the exact same ResNet process, and compare them
to our normal memory bank.

For every single patch in the test image, we calculate its Euclidean distance
(torch.cdist) to the nearest neighbor in the normal memory bank.

  - If the patch is just normal hazelnut shell, there will be a very similar
    patch in the training bank, and the minimum distance will be close to zero.
  - If the patch contains a crack, its 448-dimensional feature vector will look
    completely alien compared to anything in the normal bank, resulting in a
    massively high distance score.

d = torch.cat([torch.cdist(c, bank).min(1).values for c in patches.split(4096)])

## Post-Processing the Heatmap

The resulting distances naturally form a 128×128 heatmap of anomaly scores.
Because the original image was 1024×1024, we bilinearly upsample the heatmap
back to the original resolution.

Finally, because feature maps inherently have receptive fields that overlap, the
raw distance map can be a bit blocky or noisy. Applying a lightweight Gaussian
filter (sigma=2) smoothly blends the anomaly scores, expanding the "hot" zones
slightly to fully cover the edges of the defects. The map is then min-max
normalized to [0, 1], scaled by 255, and cast to uint8 for the final submission.

## Final Remarks

Defected Nuts proves that you do not always need massive datasets of labelled
anomalies to perform pixel-perfect segmentation. By creatively hacking a
standard pretrained classification network to extract dense, mid-level textural
descriptors, and utilizing a Nearest-Neighbor memory bank, we can perfectly map
the boundaries of "normalcy". Spotting a defect simply becomes a matter of
measuring how far an image patch wanders into the unknown.
